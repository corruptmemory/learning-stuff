## 0. The note, restated

> My general understanding is that the naturalistic fallacy is the idea that if something is natural, then it's by default good. However, this is a logical argument applied to a physical system, the world, and it's obviously false in many cases. So I need to see if that's been covered.

Two claims are packed in here.

1. **Definition:** naturalistic fallacy = "natural, therefore good."
2. **Diagnosis:** it fails because it is "obviously false in many cases."

Verdict on (1): that is the *appeal to nature*, which is one special case of what Moore meant, and Moore himself treats exactly this case in §27. So it is not a mislabel so much as a part standing in for the whole. Verdict on (2): correct, but it is the weakest of three available objections, and the one that leaves the most room for the other side to retreat.

Has it been covered? Yes, three times, at three different levels, and the interesting part is that the strongest claim is the least secure.

## 1. The one-paragraph distinction (the Plaud action item)

**Hume's is-ought gap** is a claim about *inference*: no set of premises linked only by "is" entails a conclusion linked by "ought", so every real moral argument carries a normative bridge premise, stated or suppressed. **Moore's naturalistic fallacy** is a claim about *definition*: "good" names a simple, unanalysable property, so identifying it with any other property (pleasure, evolutionary advancement, being commanded by God, being normal) is an error; the test is the open question, "granted that X is P, is X good?" **The appeal to nature** is a specific argument schema, "X is natural, therefore X is good", which is fallacious in three separable ways: its suppressed premise is false, its middle term "natural" equivocates, and no repair of the factual premise can close Hume's gap. Moore's fallacy is misnamed: it has nothing specifically to do with nature, and on the standard modern view it is not a fallacy at all but a contested metaphysical thesis.

| | Level | The error | What it rules out | Standing today |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hume 1739 | inference | ought from is alone | any moral argument with no normative premise | defensible in a restricted form; interpretation of Hume himself contested |
| Moore 1903 | definition | good = some other property | all reductive definitions of "good" | works against analytic reductions; widely held to fail against synthetic ones; "fallacy" label rejected (Frankena 1939) |
| Appeal to nature | argument schema | natural ⇒ good | one popular argument | textbook informal fallacy; uncontroversial |

## 2. Hume: the gap is in the inference

The passage, in full (T 3.1.1.27):

> In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remark'd, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I am surpriz'd to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, *is*, and *is not*, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an *ought*, or an *ought not*. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence. For as this *ought*, or *ought not*, expresses some new relation or affirmation, 'tis necessary that it shou'd be observ'd and explain'd; and at the same time that a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it.

The physics mapping is dimensional analysis. "Ought" carries a dimension that no "is" premise has. You can multiply and add dimensionless facts all day and never produce a quantity with that dimension. When a derivation appears to, the dimension was smuggled in by a bridge premise, and the bridge premise is where the actual moral content lives. Hume's complaint is that authors let the bridge premise go "imperceptible".

Three caveats that the SEP entry insists on.

- **What Hume meant is contested.** The 20th-century reading (Hare called it "Hume's Law") makes it a thesis of logic. Cognitivist readers take it more narrowly: sentiment is needed to *discover* moral properties in the first place, but once you have the concepts you can infer moral conclusions from causal premises, and Hume does so constantly. Snare's observation: on the logic reading, Hume simply assumes no factual proposition is evaluative, and never argues it.
- **Prior's counterexample (1960).** From any fact p you can validly infer "p or you ought to pay your debts." The conclusion contains an ought. Reply: the ought does no work, like a trivial solution to a differential equation. The thesis has to be restated as "no *substantive* ought from is alone", and "substantive" then needs its own account.
- **Searle's counterexample (1964).** Jones says "I promise to pay you five dollars"; therefore Jones promised; therefore Jones is under an obligation; therefore Jones ought to pay. Reply: "promise" is a thick concept whose constitutive rule ("a valid promise obligates") is itself the bridge premise, already normative.

## 3. Moore: the fallacy is in the definition

Moore's target was never "nature". It was any philosopher who thought that naming the properties that good things share was the same as defining "good". His own analogy (§10):

> Consider yellow, for example. We may try to define it, by describing its physical equivalent; we may state what kind of light-vibrations must stimulate the normal eye, in order that we may perceive it. But a moment's reflection is sufficient to shew that those light-vibrations are not themselves what we mean by yellow. […] Yet a mistake of this simple kind has commonly been made about 'good.' It may be true that all things which are good are *also* something else […] But far too many philosophers have thought that when they named those other properties they were actually defining good […] This view I propose to call the 'naturalistic fallacy'.

The instances he names: Bentham (good = pleasant), Spencer (good = more evolved), and, tellingly, T.H. Green's *metaphysical* ethics (good = some property of a supersensible reality). The third instance is why the name is wrong: the "naturalistic" fallacy is committed by non-naturalists too. The real target is reductionism.

The test is the open question (§13):

> whatever definition be offered, it may be always asked, with significance, of the complex so defined, whether it is itself good. […] whoever will attentively consider with himself what is actually before his mind when he asks the question 'Is pleasure (or whatever it may be) after all good?' can easily satisfy himself that he is not merely wondering whether pleasure is pleasant.

Physics mapping: "the light-vibrations are not what we mean by yellow" is the claim that a correlate is not a definiens. That is right about *meaning* and, on the modern view, wrong about *identity*. "Is water H2O?" was a wide-open question before chemistry, and water is H2O anyway. Openness to a competent speaker shows at most that the identity is not analytic; it does not show there is no identity. This is the paradox of analysis (Langford 1942) turned into a research programme by the Cornell realists (Boyd, Sturgeon, Brink): goodness may be a natural property discovered a posteriori, the way natural kinds are. Against *that* view the open question has no bite.

Frankena's 1939 paper made the two decisive points. First, the "fallacy" is not a fallacy. "X is pleasant, therefore X is good" is an enthymeme with the suppressed premise "whatever is pleasant is good", and charity requires reading it that way; the real disagreement is about whether that premise holds *in virtue of an identity*, which is a metaphysical dispute, not a logical slip. He proposed "definist fallacy" for the residue. Second, the open-question argument begs the question: the naturalist's position just is that the question is closed, so asserting that it is open assumes what needs proving. The SEP's summary line: "the so-called naturalistic fallacy is no fallacy at all."

The Sidgwick contrast in the SEP Moore entry is a good calibration point. Sidgwick noticed Bentham and Spencer equating goodness with a natural property and thought it a minor slip to be ignored in charity. Moore thought it vitiated their entire systems. The field has drifted back toward Sidgwick.

## 4. Moore on the appeal to nature itself (§§27–28)

This is the part that answers the note directly. Moore, in the chapter on naturalistic ethics, examines the advice "to live naturally":

> It is obvious, in the first place, that we cannot say that everything natural is good, except perhaps in virtue of some metaphysical theory […] If everything natural is equally good, then certainly Ethics, as it is ordinarily understood, disappears: for nothing is more certain, from an ethical point of view, than that some things are bad and others good.

Since disease is as natural as health, "natural" in the advice cannot mean "occurs in nature". Moore finds two things it can mean. In §27 it means **normal**: health is the normal state of an organism, and evolution "pretends to give a causal explanation of why some forms of life are normal and others are abnormal." Then:

> But is it so obvious that the normal must be good? […] Was the excellence of Socrates or of Shakespeare normal? Was it not rather abnormal, extraordinary? […] the abnormal is often better than the normal: peculiar excellence, as well as peculiar viciousness, must obviously be not normal but abnormal.

And the equivocation diagnosis, with the only joke in *Principia Ethica*:

> That health, *when* the word is used to denote something good, is good, goes no way at all to shew that health, when the word is used to denote something normal, is also good. We might as well say that, because 'bull' denotes an Irish joke and also a certain animal, the joke and the animal must be the same thing. We must not, therefore, be frightened by the assertion that a thing is natural into the admission that it is good; good does not, by definition, mean anything that is natural; and it is therefore always an open question whether anything that is natural is good.

In §28 the second sense is **necessary**: "an arbitrary minimum of what is necessary for life", the sense in which "we are advised to imitate savages and beasts." Same verdict.

So the casual definition in the note is a case Moore explicitly handled. Note *how* he handles it: not by piling up counterexamples (he concedes "I myself am not prepared to dispute that health is good") but by showing the word equivocates and the question stays open. That is a different, and stronger, objection than "obviously false in many cases."

## 5. The appeal to nature in the informal-logic literature

Schema: "That which is natural is good. N is natural. Therefore N is good." (And the mirror: unnatural, therefore bad.) Textbook example from the OK State open text: "Vaccines are unnatural, therefore they are bad for you." Fallacy Files classifies it under begging the question via loaded words, and lists "Naturalistic Fallacy" as an alias, which is how the conflation in the note got institutionalized in the first place.

The three ways it fails, in increasing strength:

1. **False premise** (soundness). "Whatever is natural is good" has counterexamples: hemlock, cancer, earthquakes; and "unnatural" counter-counterexamples: aspirin, pacemakers. This is the note's objection. It is true and cheap, and it lets the opponent retreat to "usually" or to a refined premise.
2. **Equivocation** (Moore §27). "Natural" slides between *occurs in nature*, *normal*, *necessary*, and *not man-made*, and the argument only feels persuasive because the honorific sense ("healthy") is borrowed while the descriptive sense ("normal") is asserted. No refinement of "natural" fixes this without smuggling in "good".
3. **Invalid form** (Hume). Even a true, unequivocal factual premise about naturalness cannot by itself entail "good". The bridge premise is normative and is the whole argument.

## 6. What this does to the note's diagnosis

"A logical argument applied to a physical system, and obviously false in many cases" locates the failure in the wrong place. The physical system is fine and logic is fine; the argument has a type error. It presents a normative conclusion as derived from descriptive premises, and either the derivation is invalid (Hume) or a normative premise was hidden (Frankena's enthymeme reading), and in the appeal-to-nature case the hidden premise is also false (objection 1) and equivocal (objection 2). Counterexamples show the premise is false; they do not show *why the argument form could never work*, which is the point that survives when someone patches the premise.

The ordering across the three positions is worth holding onto: the strength of the claim and its security run in opposite directions. The appeal-to-nature verdict is uncontroversial and small. Hume's Law is bigger and defensible only after retreating from "no ought from is" to "no substantive ought from is alone." Moore's is the biggest claim, that good is unanalysable and every reduction is an error, and it is the one the field now mostly rejects.

## 7. The mirror image: the moralistic fallacy

Bernard Davis, *Nature* 1978, on calls to restrict "dangerous knowledge": blocking inquiry on moral grounds is "in effect, an illogical effort to derive an 'is' from an 'ought'", and he proposed "moralistic fallacy" as "the mirror image of what Hume and G.E. Moore identified as the naturalistic fallacy." (Davis conflates Hume and Moore in that sentence; a nice specimen of the confusion this note is about.) Schema: "if X were true, something bad would follow; therefore X is false." Pinker's gloss: the naturalistic fallacy says what is found in nature is good; the moralistic fallacy says what is good is found in nature. This is the version you will actually meet in behaviour-genetics and heritability arguments. Both fallacies assume is and ought are coupled; they differ only in which way the arrow points.

## 8. Open threads

- **Is Hume's Law a theorem or a definition?** If "ought" is *defined* as non-factual, the law is trivially true and does no work (Snare). If it is not, what is the non-question-begging argument for it? The Prior and Searle cases suggest the honest statement is "we have not found a substantive is-to-ought derivation, and every candidate hides its bridge premise," which is inductive, not deductive.
- **Does the water/H2O move actually transfer to "good"?** The scientist's version of the question (competing interpretations, distinguishing observation): if goodness were a natural kind, moral inquiry should show the convergence chemistry showed. Does it? What observation would separate "goodness is a natural property we are still locating" from "goodness is not a property at all" (Mackie's error theory, or non-cognitivism, both of which also explain the open question)?
- **Where is the bridge premise in your own arguments?** Every real moral argument you make has one. Making it explicit is the whole practical payoff of this literature.

## 9. Pushback and where it landed (exchange of 2026-09-05)

The sections above were the first pass. Jim pushed back on three fronts and the discussion widened into physics and politics. Positions are recorded as stated. Where the first pass overstated, the retraction is recorded too. Of the open threads in §8, the third (where is the bridge premise) was answered in §9.5; the first two remain open.

### 9.1 Harris's dilemma and "oughts are is-es"

**Jim.** Harris's argument is persuasive. If oughts don't follow from the totality of is-es, then either there are no oughts at all, or it is necessary that oughts follow from is-es. And oughts *are* is-es: "in circumstance X one ought to do Y to get the best outcome" is simply a fact. Hume is false on this point.

**Reply.** The dilemma equivocates on "follow from."

- *Supervenience.* Oughts are fixed by the facts: no two situations identical in every natural respect differ morally. Everyone accepts this, Moore included.
- *Derivation.* Oughts cannot be deduced from descriptive premises by logic alone. A bridge premise is needed and it is normative.

These are compatible, and their compatibility is the third option the dilemma omits. A fourth is also omitted: non-cognitivism, on which oughts are not truth-apt, so they neither follow from facts nor amount to nothing. Horn (a), that there are no oughts, is Mackie's error theory, a live position with serious defenders, not a reductio.

"In circumstance X one ought to do Y to get the best outcome" is a hypothetical imperative, a means-end fact. Nobody from Hume onward denies these; Kant classed them analytic given the end. The normativity has moved into "best." Harris cashes "best" as well-being and states the bridge premise as an axiom. What Harris actually says (checked via Perplexity against *The Moral Landscape* and his essays "Facts, Values: Clarifying the Moral Landscape" and "Clarifying the Landscape"): it is "safe to begin with the premise" that the worst possible misery for everyone is bad. His critics (Pigliucci, Blackford, Ryan Born's winning essay in the Moral Landscape Challenge) all make the same observation: he supplies one normative premise and so satisfies Hume's Law rather than refuting it.

Ambiguity flagged: "there are entities that argue that..." If the point is that people's holding of oughts is a fact, that is true and beside the point. "Jim believes he ought to X" is an is about an ought.

Where Jim is right: on the synthetic-naturalist reading, moral facts just are natural facts about well-being, discovered a posteriori the way water was discovered to be H2O. Then Hume's Law is true but toothless, since "water is H2O" cannot be derived from logic either. The accurate reformulation is not "Hume is false" but "Hume is true and does not establish what it is usually deployed to establish, that morality is non-factual."

### 9.2 "The appeal to nature is trivially true in many cases"

**Jim.** "Preserving life" is aligned with, not synonymous with, "good." Not putting your hand in a fire because nature counsels against it is, in fact, good. "Good" has to cash out as something bounded.

**Reply.** Agreed that the heuristic has a high hit rate in one domain. What makes fire-avoidance good is the burn, not the counsel. Nature's counsel is defeasible evidence about the consequence. Evolved aversions were tuned to inclusive fitness in an ancestral environment, not to well-being in this one: reliable for fire, rot, heights; unreliable for sugar craving, fear of snakes but not of cars, in-group violence, sexual jealousy. The appeal gives no way to tell which case you are in without independently evaluating the consequence, which is Moore's point. Sharon Street's "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value" (2006) runs the same fact in reverse, using the fitness-not-truth origin of our intuitions to debunk realism.

A fallacy is an argument form that fails to guarantee its conclusion, not one whose conclusions are false ("it is raining, therefore 2+2=4"). Reclassify the appeal to nature: a defeasible heuristic, strong in the *prudential* domain (what is good for the organism), weak in the *moral* domain (what is good impartially). Every textbook instance of the fallacy is a moral case.

Both 9.1 and 9.2 succeed for instrumental and prudential oughts, which is where nobody contests them.

### 9.3 Frames break at the limits

**Jim.** Consequentialist and utilitarian ethics often diverge at limits. Nearly all abstracted ethical frames break down and are mutually incompatible in many cases.

**Reply.** Agreed and well documented: the repugnant conclusion, utility monsters, the demandingness objection, Kant's murderer at the door. Effective-field-theory reading: consequentialism and deontology are theories with domains of validity that agree in the interior and disagree near their cutoffs, and breakdown at the cutoff is not evidence of no physics in the interior. Named versions of this stance: Ross's prima facie duties, Berlin's value pluralism, Dancy's particularism. Alternative reading: moral concepts behave like every other natural-language concept, clear at the center and vague at the edges.

Correction: utilitarianism is a species of consequentialism, so the two cannot diverge from each other in the way described. The classic limit divergences are consequentialism against deontology, or within consequentialism, total against average utility at the repugnant conclusion.

### 9.4 "It concedes Hume's structure entirely": retracted, and why the objection still misses

**Jim.** False, and necessarily so. To even start his treatise Hume has to posit is-es that are evidence of something. "Valuing evidence" is already baked into his premises. Harris makes this point in his online replies to critics.

**Reply.** Retraction first: "concedes entirely" was too strong. Harris rejects the fact/value partition as *clean* (Putnam, *The Collapse of the Fact/Value Dichotomy*, 2002, is the academic version) while still adopting an explicit normative axiom. Two different things, run together in the first pass.

The objection does not reach Hume's claim, for three reasons.

1. *Presupposing is not premising.* Hume's Law is about entailment between propositions. "The inquirer values evidence" is about the practice of inquiry. A pragmatic presupposition of asserting is not a premise in the derivation: asserting "there are no true contradictions" presupposes non-contradiction without circularity. Hume's demand in 3.1.1.27 was that the ought be "observ'd and explain'd," made explicit, not that inquiry start from nothing.
2. *Wrong kind of ought.* Grant "one ought to proportion belief to evidence" as a premise. You can now derive epistemic oughts. You still cannot derive a moral ought without a second bridge, from epistemic to moral. The gap relocates; it does not close. Harris needs the further claim that epistemic and moral oughts are one kind, both oughts of well-being, which is the axiom again. The epistemic ought itself cashes out as a hypothetical imperative: "evidence" is the relation P(H|E), descriptive, and the norm "update by conditionalization" is justified by Dutch-book and accuracy arguments, i.e. *if* you want to avoid sure loss. Goal plus fact, the shape Jim already granted is factual.
3. *Hume applied it to himself first.* Hume did not think "believe according to evidence" was derived by reason. He thought it custom and nature. Treatise 1.4.1.7, landed on davidhume.org: "Nature, by an absolute and uncontroulable necessity has determin'd us to judge as well as to breathe and feel." On Hume's own account epistemic norms and moral norms have the same undeduced footing. What 3.1.1.27 denies is one narrow thing: that the moral ought is "a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it."

What the point does establish: there is no view from nowhere, so the naive use of Hume ("morality is subjective, science is objective") applies an asymmetry that does not exist. Harris's argument is a parity argument. It lifts ethics to the status of science or lowers science to the status of ethics, and either way leaves Hume's actual claim standing.

### 9.5 Violent agreement

**Jim.** Not a gotcha. Circling back: for Hume to even start, he had to start with facts. Is-es and oughts exist. Normative bounds on the oughts (or goals) one should pursue are added ad hoc, but even those bridge premises are not unbounded. They are constrained by what is available to human imagination and ultimately by human nature. We are in violent agreement.

**Reply.** Yes, and the agreed position is Hume's own program. The Treatise is subtitled "An Attempt to introduce the experimental Method of Reasoning into Moral Subjects," and Book 3 is an empirical account of which sentiments humans in fact have and how sympathy and the "common point of view" correct them. Modern heirs: the sentimentalists and moral psychologists (Prinz, Nichols, Haidt) and Street's Humean constructivism.

Remaining daylight, between Jim and Harris: "constrained by human nature" underdetermines. It yields a bounded *space* of livable moralities, and the Humean expects a family of local optima with no fact of the matter ranking them. Harris's landscape has one metric, well-being, and says the peaks are objectively ranked by it. Bounded pluralism against a single objective ordering.

### 9.6 Entropy and time

**Jim.** Entropy and time are correlated, not the same, given current understanding. Most of the science around them is circular because we lack a fundamental understanding of time. These are intractable at the moment; the hole at the foundations of physics is the same one as in 1988, with a long history before that.

**Reply.** Conceded: the "arrow of time" gloss in §2 of this exchange overreached by importing Boltzmann's contested identification of the time direction with the entropy gradient. Huw Price's "double standard" objection (*Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point*, 1996) is Jim's circularity charge: define "past" as the low-entropy direction, then explain why entropy rises toward the future. Loschmidt (1876) and Zermelo (1896) raised the reversibility and recurrence objections against Boltzmann. The problem of time in canonical quantum gravity, no time parameter in the Wheeler–DeWitt equation (Isham's 1992 review), predates 1988. Callender's SEP entry "Thermodynamic Asymmetry in Time" lays the whole thing out and does not resolve it.

The restricted payload survives and does not depend on what time is. The second law is not a theorem of the time-symmetric microdynamics alone; Loschmidt is the proof (reverse every velocity and the same dynamics runs entropy down). To get the law out you must add a premise the dynamics does not contain: a low-entropy boundary condition (Albert's past hypothesis, *Time and Chance*, 2000) or a measure over initial states. That is a statement about derivability within a formal system and is uncontested even by people who regard the foundations as a hole.

Twin in the Hume literature: if "ought" is *defined* as non-factual, Hume's Law is trivially true and does no work (Snare's point, via SEP), and nobody can currently say how much of the distinction is definition and how much is discovery.

### 9.7 The irony

**Jim.** I fully accept Harris's framing. That said, I am certain human nature is incompatible with it. Ironic, no?

**Reply.** Yes, and sharper than stated. The metric is defined by human nature (well-being as evolved brains experience it) and human nature cannot run the metric. Joshua Greene, *Moral Tribes* (2013): our moral machinery solved "me versus us" and is structurally incapable of "us versus them," and aggregate well-being is an us-versus-them quantity. Slovic's scope-insensitivity results: willingness to help barely moves with the number of victims; we cannot feel an aggregate, and the metric is an aggregate. Sidgwick saw the shape in 1874 and ended the *Methods of Ethics* admitting that egoism and universal benevolence are both rational and irreconcilable.

Sharper form: some peaks are built out of the things that block the climb. If well-being is what evolved brains experience as flourishing, then status, relative position, and in-group belonging are *components* of well-being, not just obstacles to it. The terrain is partly made of parochialism.

Not a contradiction. A global optimum can be well-defined and unreachable by the available optimizer; that is every fitness landscape ever drawn. A gradient-follower with bounded step size and a noisy gradient sits in a local basin, and the existence of the global peak is untouched. Ought-implies-can then says what kind of thing the metric is: a regulative ideal, a correct map of impassable terrain, not an obligation anyone is failing. Partial way out: the Madisonian one, design institutions for the humans you have (markets, law, science each extract better-than-individual outcomes from parochial agents). Partial because the institutions are built by the same agents.

### 9.8 Hossenfelder and the measurement problem

**Jim.** Sabine Hossenfelder has made a career of reminding people that playing with maths won't get us there. We need serious progress in the foundations of physics. The old bets (string theory, supersymmetry, MOND) are at best struggling. We would make massive progress if we could figure out what a measurement is.

**Reply.** Agreed on the diagnosis. *Lost in Math* (2018) is right that naturalness and beauty have produced no empirical progress since the Standard Model; SUSY at accessible scales and string theory are in the state described. MOND is a different kind of bet, phenomenological and falsifiable, and partly falsified (clusters, the Bullet Cluster), which is a form of progress the others cannot claim.

The measurement problem is the sharpest instance of the structure this whole exchange circled. Unitary Schrödinger evolution is deterministic and linear. Outcome selection and the Born rule are added by hand as a postulate the dynamics does not contain: the past hypothesis and the moral axiom again, a boundary condition the formalism will not supply. And the derivation attempt has the twin you would expect. Deutsch (1999) and Wallace (*The Emergent Multiverse*, 2012) derive the Born rule inside Everett from decision-theoretic axioms, that is, from what a rational agent *ought* to do. An is-from-ought derivation in physics. Its critics (Kent and Price, in *Many Worlds? Everett, Quantum Theory, and Reality*, OUP 2010) charge circularity: the axioms assume what the derivation delivers.

Slight push on "maths won't get us there": the measurement hole is the one experiment can reach. Objective-collapse models (GRW, Diósi–Penrose) are the only interpretations that predict something different, and the Gran Sasso underground test (Donadi et al., *Nature Physics*, 2020) constrained the Diósi–Penrose parameter space by looking for collapse-induced radiation that was not there. This may be the one foundational question settled by a detector rather than a whiteboard, which is Hossenfelder's own criterion for a question worth working on.

### 9.9 "Be wary of anyone with a plan of utopia"

**Jim.** Endless respect for Harris; his work has been indispensable for thinking clearly about many topics. But "be wary of anyone with a plan of utopia" is about as close to a universal truth as one gets. Scientific progress will inform us about better ways of living, but the result will be messy and full of conflict no matter what. There are an unbounded number of ways to use the new knowledge, which at human scale is highly provisional and rapidly revised.

**Reply.** Agreed, and the maxim follows from everything upstream rather than being a separate commitment. Pedigree: Popper (piecemeal against utopian engineering, *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, 1945; "Utopia and Violence," 1947), Berlin (the crooked timber; incommensurable goods), Hayek ("The Use of Knowledge in Society," 1945), James C. Scott (*Seeing Like a State*, 1998), Sowell (*A Conflict of Visions*, 1987; "no solutions, only trade-offs," already in Open Brain from April 2026).

- *The maxim is a stability result.* Many basins, a bounded noisy optimizer, and a provisional, rapidly revised map: a control engineer reads that as a plant with lag, measurement noise, and a non-stationary setpoint. A plan of utopia is high gain on that signal. High gain on a lagged noisy signal does not converge; it oscillates and overshoots, and the overshoots are the famines and purges. Popper's piecemeal engineering is low gain with tight feedback. Hayek stacks on top: a legible plan discards the local tacit knowledge that makes any community work, so the planner is also blind to most of the state.
- *Where the conflict lives.* Accepting Harris's framing gives facts about each axis of well-being. It gives no exchange rate between axes. Berlin's claim is that liberty against security, mercy against justice, this generation against the next are incommensurable, not merely unmeasured, so no amount of neuroscience supplies the rate because the gap is not empirical. This is the one surviving disagreement with Harris, who thinks the trade-offs are ultimately factual, and it is narrow. Facts about each good, no fact about the price. The planners are the people who claim to have the price.
- *A point for Harris.* His axiom is Popperian in form, about the floor and not the ceiling. Popper proposed replacing "the greatest happiness for the greatest number" with "the least avoidable suffering for all," on the grounds that people agree far more readily about concrete evils to remove than about goods to pursue. The utopian risk enters not with the axiom but when the landscape is treated as a plan with a known summit. Harris keeps saying there are many peaks; whether his readers hear that is another matter.
- *Messy is the generic output, not the failure mode.* A population of bounded optimizers on a multi-basin landscape with a shifting map spreads across basins and fights at the boundaries. Convergence to one peak needs a coordinator with perfect information, which Hayek rules out, or a landscape with one basin, which Berlin rules out. The knowledge is dual-use: the neuroscience that identifies well-being states identifies the levers for manufacturing them.
- Goodhart closes it. The only stable way to act on facts about better and worse is slowly, locally, with the feedback loop closed and the metric never allowed to become the target. Goodhart is already in Open Brain from the Flywheel quality-gate design, for the same reason.

### 9.10 The through-line

Ethics, thermodynamics, and quantum mechanics each have a dynamics that cannot supply its own boundary condition (the moral axiom, the past hypothesis, the projection postulate and Born rule), a bridge posited and bounded by something outside the formalism (human nature, the actual early universe), and a literature of derivation attempts that all draw the circularity charge (Frankena on Moore's open question, Price on Boltzmann, Kent and Price on Deutsch–Wallace). The Hume note turned out to be about that.
